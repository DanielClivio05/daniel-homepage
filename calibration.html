Calibrating a ranking against someone else's

StackedRoster computes its own ranking for Division I college tennis and is supposed to track the official ITA list closely without copying it. Those two goals pull against each other, and most of the engineering lives in that tension.

The ITA publishes a weekly ranking and, alongside it, a points total for each player. It does not publish the function that produced those points. So the problem isn't ranking players — it's ranking them in a way that lands close to a target I can see the output of but not the mechanism behind, using only data that will still exist next Tuesday when the job runs unattended.

What shipped is two stages. The first ranks everybody. The second re-ranks the part that matters most, and exists only because the first one wasn't good enough at the top.

Stage one: a convergence graph

Every match result is an edge. A player's score is the sum of their ten best win points, divided by a calibrated base plus the weight of their losses. The ten-win cap isn't my invention — the ITA documents it — and it makes the score a quality average rather than a volume total, so someone with four elite wins can outrank someone with twenty ordinary ones.

score = (your ten best win points) ÷ (calibrated base + weighted losses)

The circularity is the point. A win is worth the opponent's score from the previous iteration, so every score depends on every other score, and the whole thing has to be iterated until it settles — in practice until no score moves by more than 0.001. Each pass is normalised to a mean of one, because the raw map is a contraction and without renormalisation the entire table drifts toward zero.

Two smaller weights matter more than I expected. Losing to a much weaker opponent costs slightly more than losing to a peer, which stops a strong record being assembled out of avoided risk. And a road win is worth a bit more than a home one. Walkovers are dropped entirely; retirements, defaults and injuries are kept, because those are matches that happened.

Where it wasn't enough

The graph is respectable everywhere and slightly soft exactly where people look. Measured against two pinned ITA lists — I froze the 30 April and 20 May 2026 publications so I'd have a stationary target — the raw graph sat around three places off through the top 25 and five through the top 50. For a ranking whose entire claim is that it tracks the official one, being five places out inside the top 50 is the difference between interesting and useful.

Stage two: recovering a points table

The ITA's published points total is the one field that leaks information about their model. So I fitted a points table against it: a player's predicted points are the sum, over their wins, of a band value based on how highly ranked the opponent was, plus a bonus for beating someone ranked above them, plus a flat term per loss. Fourteen opponent bands, finer at the top where a single place matters more, and five upset bands.

The trap here took me a while to see. The obvious thing is to fit using the ITA's own opponent ranks, which produces a much better fit — and a model that cannot run. The ITA's opponent ranks aren't available live, so a model that depends on them can only ever be evaluated retrospectively against the thing it's trying to predict. It's circular in a way that flatters the numbers and ships nothing. The fitted coefficients are therefore fitted on my converged opponent ranks, which is worse on paper and is the only version that can actually run on a Tuesday.

Why only the top eighty

Re-ranking the entire pool with the points model makes it worse. Fit across roughly a thousand players and the win-band values go negative at the weak end — the model starts concluding that beating a poor opponent should cost you, which is an artefact of the fit rather than anything true about tennis. So the points model re-ranks the top 80 and the convergence graph keeps the order below that, where it was better anyway. Every player in the ITA's top 50 lands inside my top 84, so the band is wide enough to catch what it needs to.

Two models with a seam in the middle is uglier than one model. It's also what the data supported, and I'd rather ship the honest hybrid than a single elegant model that's worse.

The top-50 figure is where the work paid off: roughly a place better through the top 25, and a full place or more through the top 50.

Why the site shows a live comparison instead of a number

I could put "within 2.6 places of the official ranking" on the homepage. I decided not to, because it isn't stable. The ITA re-converges its whole list on every publish, so my deviation wobbles week to week through no change of mine. A fixed accuracy claim would be true on the day I measured it and quietly wrong afterwards.

So the site publishes a live panel comparing my current list to the current official one. It's a worse marketing decision and a better one for anyone deciding whether to trust the thing. It also means that when I'm wrong, I'm wrong in public, which has been a useful discipline.

The part I haven't solved

The residual error is loss-driven. Players with clean records rank a touch low; players carrying many losses rank a touch high. The obvious candidate is a steeper, convex loss term — and every version I've tried overfits. The top-25 sample is about 124 rows, which is small enough that tuning a loss curve against it mostly learns those particular rows. Weighting losses by opponent strength helps the top 50 and hurts the top 25.

It's an open lever rather than a defect, and I'd rather write that down than let it sit as an unexplained wobble in the comparison panel.

The fitted coefficients aren't published. The structure above is the whole idea; the numbers are calibration and they change. StackedRoster's public methodology page carries the version written for readers who want the ranking explained rather than the engineering.
