Project

StackedRoster

An independent ranking system for NCAA Division I college tennis, live at stackedroster.net.

College tennis has an official ranking, published weekly by the ITA. It's the thing everybody quotes and almost nobody can explain. I wanted to know whether I could reconstruct something that behaved like it from the match results alone — and then, because the honest version of that experiment is a public one, publish my list beside theirs and let people see the gaps.

It covers men's and women's Division I, updates between the ITA's weekly posts, and ranks past their published top 125. I built it and I run it; there's nobody else on it.

How it's put together

The ranking itself runs in two stages. The first is a PageRank-style convergence graph over every match result — your score comes from your ten best wins, divided by a calibrated base plus your losses, where the value of a win is the opponent's score from the previous iteration. It's circular by construction, so it's iterated until nothing moves more than 0.001. The second stage re-ranks the top band using a points table fitted against the ITA's own published points.

Three decisions I'd defend

It is not the ITA ranking, and it says so everywhere

The easiest way to make an independent ranking look authoritative is to be vague about what it is. I went the other way: the site states plainly that this is StackedRoster's own list, links out to the official ITA rankings as the authority, publishes a plain-language methodology page, and shows a live panel comparing my list to theirs. Publishing my own error is the part that makes the rest of it worth reading.

Nothing publishes itself

The news agent drafts short articles from week-to-week ranking movements using the Anthropic API. Every draft lands in a review queue in /admin and waits for a person. No article auto-publishes, and no automated process is allowed to mutate a roster. A ranking system that generates its own coverage of itself is exactly where an unattended model does damage, and the cost of a human gate here is a few minutes a week.

These are real people, most of them young

The whole system is built on named, identifiable athletes, many of them teenagers, who never asked to be ranked by me. I took an explicit position on that rather than leaving it implicit: only competition results, a documented correction route, and a documented way to be removed. It's the constraint I thought hardest about and the one that shaped what the product does not do.

What I'd do differently

The ranking tables should render on the server. Right now they're fetched from Supabase in the browser, so the HTML a crawler receives says Loading… where the ranking should be. That costs me search visibility, hurts anyone on a slow connection, and it's the first thing I'd change — the data is public and static between refreshes, which is precisely the case server rendering exists for.

The calibration constants live in environment variables. That made tuning fast while I was fitting the model, and it means a published list can't be fully reconstructed from the repository alone — you'd also need to know what the environment held that day. Versioned config rows in the database, stamped onto each published list, would have been the better call and I'd make it now.

The residual error is loss-driven and I haven't solved it. Players with clean records rank slightly low and players with many losses rank slightly high. The obvious fix is a steeper loss term, but every attempt overfits a top-25 sample of about 124 rows, and the version that helps the top 50 makes the top 25 worse. I'd rather say that plainly than quote an accuracy number and hope nobody checks.
